A calibrated world model of first-name and last-name pairs,
and its quantization

anagraname · technical report · 24 October 2026
Abstract. We model the probability that a person in country $c$ has the first name $f$ and the last name $l$, for 105 countries, 1.7 M first names and 3.5 M last names. The baseline is the independence model $p(f\mid c)\,p(l\mid c)$. We add a low-rank interaction between names with per-country gates, train it with an importance-sampled estimate of the partition function, and fit a scale and an offset per country on held-out people. The model is better than independence in all 105 countries, with a test gain of @@gain_cal@@ bits per person. Product quantization with one byte for each pair of dimensions reduces the name vectors from @@mb_fp32@@ MB to @@mb_pq2@@ MB, for a loss of @@loss_pq2@@ bits per person. All countries stay above the baseline.

1 Introduction

An anagram tool for person names must rank many candidate pairs $(f, l)$ by how plausible they are in a given country. The independence model is a strong baseline, because most of the information is in the frequencies of the two names. The interaction between first and last names is weaker, but it exists: names from the same language or community occur together more frequently than independence predicts.

We have two requirements. (i) The model must not be worse than the baseline in any country: a good world average can hide losses in small countries. (ii) The model must be small enough for a web browser. Sections 3 to 5 give the model, its training and its calibration, which satisfy (i). Section 7 gives the quantization, which satisfies (ii).

2 Data and evaluation

The data is name_dataset [1], restricted to names in Latin script after NFC normalization and to names that occur 5 or more times in the world. It has 187 M unique triples $(c, f, l)$ with a count of people $n_{cfl}$. For each triple we draw $n^{\text{test}} \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n_{cfl}, 0.1)$ and $n^{\text{valid}} \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n_{cfl} - n^{\text{test}}, 1/9)$, which gives 282 M train, 35 M validation and 35 M test people. The metric is the negative log-likelihood per person and the gain over the baseline $p_0$:

$$L = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log_2 p(f_i, l_i \mid c_i), \qquad G = L_0 - L. \tag{1}$$

The baseline smooths the marginals of each country toward the world marginal $\pi$ with $\alpha$ pseudo-people:

$$p_0(f, l \mid c) = \hat p(f \mid c)\,\hat p(l \mid c), \qquad \hat p(f \mid c) = \frac{N_{fc} + \alpha\,\pi_f}{N_c + \alpha}, \tag{2}$$

where $N_{fc}$ counts the train people of country $c$ with first name $f$ (same for $l$). The value $\alpha = 10^5$ is the best on the validation split. The baseline has $L_0 = @@null_bits@@$ bits per person on the test split.

3 Model

The model adds an interaction to the baseline, only on the pairs whose two names occur in the train people of the country:

$$\log p(f, l \mid c) = \log p_0(f, l \mid c) + \mathbb{1}[(f, l) \in S_c]\,\bigl(\beta_c\, s_c(f, l) + b_c\bigr) - \log Z_c, \tag{3}$$

with the score $s_c(f, l) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} g_{cj}\, u_{fj}\, v_{lj}$. The vectors $u_f, v_l \in \mathbb{R}^k$, $k = 16$, are the same in all countries. Only names with $m = 20$ or more train people have a vector: 1.34 M of the 5.18 M names. The other names have $u = 0$. The gates $g_c \in (0, 1)^k$ let each country use a subset of the dimensions. The set $S_c = F_c \times L_c$ contains the pairs of names seen in country $c$; out of $S_c$ the model is the baseline. The scale $\beta_c$ and the offset $b_c$ come from the calibration (Section 5); during training, $\beta_c = 1$ and $b_c = 0$. The partition function is

$$Z_c = 1 - P_c + \sum_{(f, l) \in S_c} p_0(f, l \mid c)\, e^{\beta_c s_c(f, l) + b_c}, \qquad P_c = p_0(S_c \mid c). \tag{4}$$

The sum has $\sum_c |F_c|\,|L_c| = 3.3 \times 10^{12}$ terms. We compute it exactly, in blocks, with MLX [2] on an Apple M4 Max GPU in about 3 minutes. All results in this report use the exact $Z_c$.

4 Training

The training loss is the negative log-likelihood of the train people (in nats). The baseline terms are constant, thus

$$\ell = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i} s_{c_i}(f_i, l_i) + \sum_c w_c \log Z_c, \tag{5}$$

where $w_c$ is the fraction of people from country $c$. The sum in (4) is too large for each step. We write $Z_c = 1 - P_c + P_c\, \mathbb{E}_{p_0(\cdot \mid S_c)}[e^{s}]$ and estimate the expectation by self-normalized importance sampling [7], with $J = 4096$ samples per country and per step:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_{p_0(\cdot \mid S_c)}[e^{s}] &\approx \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{J} \omega_j\, e^{s_j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{J} \omega_j}, \qquad \omega_j = \frac{p_0(f_j, l_j \mid S_c)}{q_c(f_j, l_j)},\\ q_c &= \tfrac13 \bigl(\hat p_{\text{train}}(\cdot \mid c) + p_0(\cdot \mid S_c) + t_c \otimes t_c\bigr). \end{aligned} \tag{6}$$

The first part of the proposal samples train people, where $e^{s}$ is large. The second part is the target. The third part samples each name of country $c$ in proportion to its number of distinct pairs, which gives more weight to rare names. Because $q_c \ge p_0(\cdot \mid S_c)/3$, all weights satisfy $\omega_j \le 3$.

Two other choices failed. With sampled softmax (InfoNCE [3], 16 negatives), 19 countries were below the baseline, also on the train split: the interaction was too strong, and the cause was not overfitting. Without the threshold $m$, names with 3 to 9 people received large vectors under Adam, with scores up to 28 on pairs that no sample sees, and the estimate (6) was wrong. With $m = 20$, it agrees with the exact $\log Z_c$ within 0.002 in the countries that we checked. Settings: batches of 65,536 people, $10^4$ steps of AdamW (learning rate $10^{-2}$, weight decay 0.1), @@train_min@@ minutes in total.

5 Calibration

For each country we fit $(\beta_c, b_c)$ on the validation people. With $E = \mathbb{E}_{\text{valid}}[s\, \mathbb{1}_S]$ and $Q = P_{\text{valid}}(S_c)$, the validation gain in nats is

$$G_c(\beta, b) = \beta E + b\, Q - \log\bigl(1 - P_c + e^{b} M_c(\beta)\bigr), \qquad M_c(\beta) = \sum_{(f, l) \in S_c} p_0(f, l \mid c)\, e^{\beta s_c(f, l)}. \tag{7}$$

For a fixed $\beta$, $G_c$ is concave in $b$, with its maximum at

$$e^{b^\star} = \frac{Q\,(1 - P_c)}{(1 - Q)\, M_c(\beta)}. \tag{8}$$

One exact pass per country gives the histogram of $s$ weighted by $p_0$ (bins of width 0.01). This gives $M_c(\beta)$ for all $\beta$ in $\{0, 0.01, \dots, 2\}$, and we keep the best pair, with $b$ clipped to $[-3, 3]$. Because $(\beta, b) = (0, 0)$ is the baseline, the fitted validation gain is never negative. This is a per-country form of temperature scaling [4]. The median $\beta_c$ is @@beta_median@@, and $\beta_c < 1$ in @@beta_lt1@@ of the 105 countries: the trained interaction is too strong in most countries.

7 Quantization

Only the name vectors need compression: $U$ and $V$ have 1.34 M rows of $k = 16$ numbers, @@mb_fp32@@ MB in float32. The gates and offsets are $105 \times 17$ numbers. A bit mask of the 5.18 M names (0.65 MB) tells which names have a vector.

7.1 Cost of an error on the vectors

Quantization changes the scores by $\delta = \hat s - s$. For one country, the change of the loss is exactly

$$\Delta L = -\,\mathbb{E}_{\text{data}}[\delta] + \log \mathbb{E}_{p}\bigl[e^{\delta}\bigr] = \bigl(\mathbb{E}_{p}[\delta] - \mathbb{E}_{\text{data}}[\delta]\bigr) + \tfrac12 \operatorname{Var}_{p}(\delta) + O(\delta^3), \tag{9}$$

where $p$ is the model. Let $\varepsilon_f = \hat u_f - u_f$ and $\eta_l = \hat v_l - v_l$. To first order, the first term of (9) is $\sum_f \varepsilon_f^{\top} \nabla_{u_f} \ell + \sum_l \eta_l^{\top} \nabla_{v_l} \ell$, which is zero at a stationary point of the likelihood. If the errors of different names are independent with mean zero,

$$\Delta L \approx \tfrac12 \sum_f p(f)\, \varepsilon_f^{\top} A_f\, \varepsilon_f + \tfrac12 \sum_l p(l)\, \eta_l^{\top} B_l\, \eta_l, \qquad A_f = \mathbb{E}_p\bigl[(\beta g \odot v_l)(\beta g \odot v_l)^{\top} \bigm| f\bigr], \tag{10}$$

and $B_l$ is the same with the roles of $u$ and $v$ exchanged. The cost of an error on a name is proportional to the frequency of the name. With $A_f \approx a I$, all the methods below minimize the error weighted by the count $w_i$ of train people of each name $i$ (row $W_i$ of $U$ or $V$):

$$D(\hat W) = \sum_i w_i\, \lVert \hat W_i - W_i \rVert^2. \tag{11}$$

Equation (10) is an approximation: the weight decay and the calibration move the model away from the stationary point.

7.2 Scalar quantization

Each dimension $j$ has one scale. With $b$ bits and $r = 2^{b-1} - 1$ [5],

$$\Delta_j = \frac{\gamma_j \max_i |W_{ij}|}{r}, \qquad q_{ij} = \operatorname{clip}\Bigl(\operatorname{round}\frac{W_{ij}}{\Delta_j},\, -r,\, r\Bigr), \qquad \hat W_{ij} = \Delta_j\, q_{ij}, \tag{12}$$

where the clip factor $\gamma_j \in \{0.4, 0.5, \dots, 1\}$ minimizes the term $j$ of (11). Without clipping, the error is uniform on $[-\Delta_j/2, \Delta_j/2]$ with variance $\Delta_j^2/12$, thus by (10) $\Delta L \propto \Delta^2$. From 8 to 4 bits, $\Delta_j$ increases by about $127/7 \approx 18$, and (10) predicts a factor of about 330 on $\Delta L$. We measure @@loss_int8@@ and @@loss_int4@@ bits per person: a factor of @@ratio_int@@. The cost is $bk/8$ bytes per name.

7.3 Product quantization

Product quantization [6] cuts the $k$ dimensions into $k/g$ groups of $g$. Each group $m$ has a codebook $C^{(m)} \in \mathbb{R}^{256 \times g}$, and each name stores one byte per group:

$$a_{im} = \arg\min_{a}\, \bigl\lVert W_i^{(m)} - C_a^{(m)} \bigr\rVert^2, \qquad \hat W_i = \bigl(C^{(1)}_{a_{i1}}, \dots, C^{(k/g)}_{a_{i,k/g}}\bigr). \tag{13}$$

The codebooks minimize (11) by weighted Lloyd iterations, from 256 rows drawn with probability proportional to $w_i$ (25 iterations):

$$C_a^{(m)} \leftarrow \frac{\sum_{i:\, a_{im} = a} w_i\, W_i^{(m)}}{\sum_{i:\, a_{im} = a} w_i}. \tag{14}$$

The rate is $8/g$ bits per dimension. For $g = 2$ it is the same as 4-bit scalar quantization, but the 256 centroids follow the weighted joint density of the two dimensions, while the scalar grid puts its $15 \times 15$ cells on a square, many of them in empty regions. The loss is @@loss_pq2@@ bits per person, against @@loss_int4@@ for 4-bit scalar quantization. With $g = 4$ and $g = 8$ the loss is @@loss_pq4@@ and @@loss_pq8@@.

7.4 Truncation

The last method keeps the vectors of the $n$ most frequent names only. The other names share the weighted mean $\bar W = \sum_i w_i W_i / \sum_i w_i$, which keeps their mean interaction. By (10), these names have a small $p(f)$, thus their error costs little. With 4-bit vectors for the top 50 % of the names (@@mb_top50@@ MB), the loss is @@loss_top50@@, less than product quantization with $g = 4$ at the same size.

7.5 Choice

Figure 2 shows the trade-off. Float16 and int8 have no measurable loss. At 11 MB, product quantization with $g = 2$ is better than 4-bit scalar quantization. Below 6 MB, truncation is the best method. We choose product quantization with $g = 2$, the smallest variant with a loss of 0.01 bits per person or less. The file, with codebooks, bit masks, gates and offsets, has @@file_mb@@ MB. Its exact test gain is @@gain_file@@ bits per person, and no country is below the baseline. The smallest variant (@@mb_small@@ MB) still has a gain of @@gain_small@@, more than the float32 model trained with sampled softmax.

8 Limitations

The sizes do not include the count tables of the baseline, which the browser also needs. We did not test product quantization together with truncation. Some model choices were made with the test results visible, thus a small selection bias is possible.
