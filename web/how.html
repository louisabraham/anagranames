How it works

from 491 million records to your anagrams.

  1. 491,655,925 records in name_dataset, from 105 countries
  2. 352,931,936 people kept: Latin script, names of 5 people or more
  3. 5,180,098 names: 1,724,039 first names, 3,456,059 last names
  4. 154,775,215 real full names, with their people in each country
  5. A few ms to find the anagrams of your name, in your browser

What does the page do?

You type your name. The page finds the full names (one first name and one last name) that use exactly the same letters. For Yuki Tanaka, it finds Yuki Nakata, Tanaka Yuki and 32,432 other names.

The page shows two kinds of people, and a switch chooses one:

Real peoplePeople in the data have this full name. The page counts them.
Synthetic peopleNobody in the data has this full name. A model computes its probability.

A globe and a list of the countries show where these people are. A click on a country shows its people only.

Where do the names come from?

From name_dataset [1]: 491,655,925 records (first name, last name, country) from the Facebook leak of 2021. We keep the names in Latin script that at least 5 people have in the world. This removes most typing errors and fake names. It is the only filter: the page keeps all these names, in each country where they occur.

The data is a sample of accounts, not a census. One person with two accounts counts two times, and the use of Facebook is not the same in all countries. For this reason the page says “at least 41 people”.

For France, you can also use INSEE [2, 3]: the first names given to the children born in France from 1900 to 2022 (33,234 names, 86.4 million births), and the last names of the people born in France from 1891 to 2000 (215,906 names, 68.0 million people). INSEE counts each name alone, so it has no real full names.

How does the page find the anagrams?

Each name becomes its letters A to Z, without accents, spaces or hyphens: Jean-Paul becomes JEANPAUL, Łukasz becomes LUKASZ. Two names are anagrams when they have the same letters.

The names are in a tree of their sorted letters. The search goes down the tree of first names and enters a branch only if your name still has its letter. For each first name, the letters that remain give the last names. The tree puts the rarest letters (Q, X, Z…) at the top, so the search skips most of the names at once. A search takes a few milliseconds.

How probable is a full name?

A model gives the probability that a person in a country has this full name. It has two parts:

  1. The two names alone. The share of people with the first name in the country, times the share of people with the last name.
  2. The pair. Some names go together more than chance: a Japanese first name with a Japanese last name, a Portuguese first name with a Portuguese last name. Each name has 16 numbers, learned from 282 million people, and the model compares the numbers of the two names.

“1 in N” is the share of people that the model expects to have the name. Yuki Tanaka is 1 in 13,000 in Japan. Japan has 125 million people, so the model expects about 9,400 people with this name in Japan.

For the world, the page mixes the countries by their population (World Bank, 2023), not by the number of accounts in the data: Japan has 1.9% of the population of the 105 countries, but only 0.09% of the accounts.

Show the math

For a first name $f$, a last name $l$ and a country $c$, the base is:

$$p_0(f,l\mid c)=p(f\mid c)\,p(l\mid c),\qquad p(f\mid c)=\frac{N_{fc}+\alpha\,\pi_f}{N_c+\alpha}$$

with $N_{fc}$ the people of $c$ with the name $f$, $N_c$ the people of $c$, $\pi_f$ the share of $f$ in the world and $\alpha=10^5$. The model adds the pair term:

$$\log p(f,l\mid c)=\log p_0(f,l\mid c)+\mathbb{1}[(f,l)\in S_c]\,\bigl(\beta_c\,u_f^\top \operatorname{diag}(g_c)\,v_l+b_c\bigr)-\log Z_c$$

$u_f$ and $v_l$ have 16 numbers each. The weights $g_c$, the scale $\beta_c$ and the offset $b_c$ belong to the country. $S_c$ contains the pairs whose two names occur in $c$, and $Z_c$ makes the probabilities sum to 1. In the world, with $P_c$ the population of $c$ and $P$ the population of the 105 countries:

$$p(f,l)=\sum_c \frac{P_c}{P}\,p(f,l\mid c),\qquad p(c\mid f,l)=\frac{P_c\,p(f,l\mid c)}{P\,p(f,l)}$$

The expected people with the full name are $P\,p(f,l)$ in the world and $P_c\,p(f,l\mid c)$ in the country $c$.

How was the model trained?

0.467bits per person better than the two names alone, on 35 million people that the model did not see
105 of 105countries are better than the two names alone (the lowest: Uruguay)
2.7 minof training on a laptop GPU (Apple M4 Max)

We split the people at random: 282 million to train, 35 million to adjust, 35 million to test. The model learns the 16 numbers of each name that at least 20 people have (1.34 million names). The other names use the base only.

The difficult part is $Z_c$: it adds 3.3 million million pairs. At each step of training, the model estimates it from 4,096 sample pairs per country. At the end, a GPU computes it exactly, in about 3 minutes.

Then each country gets its own scale and offset for the pair term, fitted on the 35 million people kept to adjust. A scale of 0 gives the base, so the fit never makes a country worse than the base on these people. Each step helps:

Show the math

The loss is the negative log-likelihood of the train people. The base terms are constant, thus

$$\ell=-\frac1N\sum_i s_{c_i}(f_i,l_i)+\sum_c w_c\log Z_c,\qquad Z_c=1-P_c+\sum_{(f,l)\in S_c}p_0(f,l\mid c)\,e^{s_c(f,l)}$$

with $w_c$ the share of the train people in $c$ and $P_c=p_0(S_c\mid c)$. With $Z_c=1-P_c+P_c\,\mathbb E_{p_0(\cdot\mid S_c)}[e^s]$, the expectation is estimated by self-normalized importance sampling [5] with $J=4096$ samples per country and step, from a mix of three proposals (the train people, the base on $S_c$, and the names of $c$ in proportion to their distinct pairs), so that every weight is at most 3. Training: $10^4$ steps of AdamW, batches of 65,536 people, learning rate $10^{-2}$, weight decay 0.1.

Calibration: for each country, with $E=\mathbb E_{\text{valid}}[s\,\mathbb 1_S]$ and $Q=P_{\text{valid}}(S_c)$, the validation gain

$$G_c(\beta,b)=\beta E+bQ-\log\bigl(1-P_c+e^bM_c(\beta)\bigr),\qquad M_c(\beta)=\sum_{(f,l)\in S_c}p_0(f,l\mid c)\,e^{\beta s_c(f,l)}$$

has its best offset at $e^{b^\star}=Q(1-P_c)/\bigl((1-Q)M_c(\beta)\bigr)$. One pass per country gives $M_c(\beta)$ for all $\beta$ in $\{0,0.01,\dots,2\}$. The median $\beta_c$ is 0.86: the trained pair term is too strong in most countries [4].

How does it fit in a browser?

The 16 numbers of the 1.34 million names take 86 MB. The page stores each pair of numbers as one of 256 values, in one byte [6]: 11 MB, for a loss of 0.0056 bits per person.

The page does not download the full data. It downloads the tree of the names first, then only the files of the names that your search finds, and one file with the real names of your letters. It keeps the files for the next searches.

Show the comparison

Loss against the 32-bit numbers, in bits per person: 8-bit numbers 0.000048, 4-bit numbers 0.010, one byte for 2 numbers 0.0056 (chosen), one byte for 4 numbers 0.049, one byte for 8 numbers 0.120. With 4-bit numbers for the most frequent half of the names only (5.4 MB), the loss is 0.021. All these choices keep the 105 countries better than the base.

Counted or computed?

The page never mixes the two:

Counted in the dataThe people of name_dataset with the name. “41 people” means 41 accounts.
Computed by the model“1 in 700,000” and “≈ 180 people expected” are estimates from the frequency of each name in each country.

The two can disagree. Four of the 11 accounts named Louis Abraham are in the United States, and none is in Nigeria. But the model puts the largest share of the expected people (23%) in Nigeria: the data has 1,601 people named Louis and 4,601 named Abraham there, and Nigeria has 3.5% of the population of the 105 countries.

What do the servers see?

The search runs in your browser: the page does not send your name. But it downloads the files of the names made of your letters, so the server of the site can see which files, thus which letters your name can contain. The files contain the names and their people per country, and nothing else about the people.

Limits

  • name_dataset comes from profiles of a social network: it has typing errors, nicknames and names from other countries.
  • Only names in Latin script are kept.
  • INSEE covers only the people born in France, for the periods above.
  • The probabilities are estimates of a model. Some choices of the model were made with the test results visible, so a small bias is possible.
